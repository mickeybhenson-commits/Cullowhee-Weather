A second opinion on Cullowhee Creek

This page shows what FLASH — a national flash-flood model run by NOAA's National Severe Storms Laboratory — makes of this watershed right now.

Why an outside model

FLASH covers the whole country at one-kilometre resolution and knows nothing about Cullowhee Creek in particular. That is exactly the point: when a national model and NOAH's own engine agree, the agreement means something.

What you are looking at

Each sub-basin is filled by its status on the same colour ladder the live map uses — green normal through red significant. The table lists the number behind each colour. Switch on FLASH grid to see the raw model field underneath.

How to read it

Value is the model's number for the selected layer. Reading is where that number falls against thresholds the National Weather Service publishes — for unit streamflow, 2.0 means flash flooding likely and 6.0 significant.

What it is not

This is modeled, not measured. It can support a watch; it never raises a warning on its own — that still needs a measured stream rise. It also runs 0–60 minutes behind, so it corroborates after the fact rather than buying lead time.

FLASH is driven by radar rainfall, and these mountains sit in a known radar gap — a quiet reading is weaker evidence than a loud one. Colour appears only where there is something to see, so an empty map is a real answer. Source: MRMS CONUS FLASH via THREDDS WMS at thredds.aos.wisc.edu, a university server rather than a NOAA endpoint.

About this layer ▸

An independent second opinion. NOAA's National Severe Storms Laboratory runs FLASH — a hydrologic model covering the whole country at one-kilometre resolution, driven by MRMS radar rainfall. It knows nothing about Cullowhee Creek in particular, and that is the point: when a national model and NOAH's own engine agree, the agreement means something.

Unit streamflow is modeled flow per unit of drainage area, so it is scale-free — a half-square-mile headwater and the full watershed are judged against the same published number. NWS thresholds: 2.0 m³ s⁻¹ km⁻² flash flooding likely, 6.0 significant. QPE/FFG above 100% increasingly likely; above 120% captured 75% of observed flash-flood reports.

Modeled, not measured. This can support a watch. It never raises a warning on its own — that still requires a measured stream rise.

How current. FLASH steps every 10 minutes, but the files are packaged hourly and post near the end of the hour they cover, and this feed republishes every 30 minutes. Expect 0–60 minutes behind. Headwater lead times here are about half an hour, so treat this as corroboration after the fact, not early warning.

Radar caveat. This watershed sits in the KGSP beam-blockage gap. A quiet reading is weaker evidence than a loud one. Colour appears only where there is something to see — an empty map is a real answer.

Source: MRMS CONUS FLASH via THREDDS WMS at thredds.aos.wisc.edu — a university server, not a NOAA endpoint. Fine for a cross-check; not for anything load-bearing.
